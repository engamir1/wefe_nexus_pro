Series. It is written to serve two audiences simultaneously: first, as a standalone investment -grade project 
report on drainage -water reuse in Egypt; and second, as a worked template that trainees and project teams

deliberate: it demonstrates how to ground each section of the template in verifiab le, cited evidence instead of 
generic placeholder text, which is the standard trainees should apply to their own case studies.

Page 4 of 21 1. Executive Summary  
Egypt's freshwater endowment has fallen to roughly 560 m³ per capita per year — well below the 1,000 m³ 
water -poverty threshold and closer still to the 500 m³ absolute -scarcity line — as the Nile's fixed annual 
allocation of about 55.5 billion cubic metres  (BCM) is stretched across a population that has passed 106 million. 
Agricultural drainage water, historically treated as a disposal problem, has become one of the country's few 
expandable water resources: the drainage network carries in excess of 14 BCM p er year, most of it discharged 
untreated into lakes, drains and the Mediterranean.  
Rather than a hypothetical pipeline concept, this report is built around a programme Egypt is already 
constructing at national scale. Between roughly 2018 and 2025 the Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation 
(MWRI), together with the Armed Forces Engine ering Authority, commissioned three mega -plants that 
together define the country's drainage -water -reuse model:  
• Bahr El -Baqar WWTP (Sinai / east of the Suez Canal) — treatment capacity of about 5.6 million m³/day, 
supporting reclamation of roughly 456,000 feddans.  
• Al-Mahsama WWTP (Ismailia, east bank of the Suez Canal) — capacity of about 0.365 BCM/year, 
supporting reclamation of roughly 70,000 feddans.  
• New Delta / El -Hammam WWTP (Western Desert) — the largest of the three and among the largest 
water -treatment plants in the world, with a design capacity in the range of 2.2 BCM/year (roughly 6 
million m³/day), supporting reclamation targeted at up to 1.5 m illion feddans.  
Egyptian officials have indicated that, taken together, these three plants are expected to deliver on the order 
of 5 BCM of treated drainage water per year by 2026 — a volume comparable to a major new river allocation, 
generated entirely from water Egypt w as previously discharging as waste. This report uses that operating 
programme as the evidence base for a fourth, replicable project archetype: decentralised, solar -powered 
drainage -water desalination and reuse units that can be deployed across the Nile Del ta's secondary drainage 
network to extend the same logic to medium -scale, farmer -facing infrastructure.  
The proposed project — sized here at USD 670 million in an illustrative configuration — combines drainage 
water collection, reverse -osmosis desalination, 150 MW of solar generation, an irrigation distribution network, 
constructed wetlands and a digital wat er-management platform. It is structured for blended finance, aligns 
directly with Egypt Vision 2030 and the National Climate Change Strategy 2050, and is assessed here as 
investment -ready pending a full feasibility study, with a WEFE Nexus composite score  of 95/100.  
Three findings distinguish this report from a conventional pre -feasibility note. First, the technology and delivery 
risk are already substantially retired: Egypt has proven, at national scale and within a single decade, that mega -
scale drainage treatment a nd inter -basin conveyance (including the New Delta project's underground pipeline 
system carrying water tens of kilometres into the Western Desert) can be engineered, financed and operated. 
Second, the resource itself is abundant and currently undervalued:  at over 14 BCM/year of total drainage flow, 
the volume already treated by the three flagship plants — on the order of 5 BCM/year by 2026 — still 
represents well under half of the theoretically recoverable stream, leaving substantial headroom for a 
decentr alised second wave of investment such as the project proposed here. Third, the social and 
environmental risk profile is well documented rather than speculative: independent field research on Al -
Mahsama has already identified the specific mechanism by which  drainage reallocation can affect old -land 
smallholders, which allows this report to design safeguards against a known risk rather than a hypothetical 
one.  
The remainder of this report is organised as a full, investable project document — background, WEFE Nexus 
analysis, technical description, stakeholder mapping, climate resilience, logframe, screening and bankability, 
financials, capital structure, risk, fu nding -call fit, MRV, safeguards, expected impacts, readiness, investor pitch

3. Project Background and Rationale  
Egypt receives under 600 m³ per capita per year of water, against a Nile allocation fixed by the 1959 agreement 
at 55.5 BCM/year for Egypt — a volume unchanged for over six decades while population has more than 
doubled. The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam  (GERD) filling has added further hydrological uncertainty 
upstream. Key structural challenges include water scarcity, seawater and drain -water salinity intrusion into the 
Delta aquifer, climate -driven variability, stagnating agricultural productivity per unit of water, and rising energy 
costs for conventional treatment.  
Egypt's response, branded domestically as Irrigation System 2.0, has centred on treating agricultural drainage 
water as a resource rather than a waste stream. The three flagship plants already reallocate treated drainage 
water from the Delta's 'old lands' to newly reclaimed desert -fringe 'new lands' in Sinai, around the Suez Canal, 
and in the Western Desert — a model this report proposes to replicate at decentralised, solar -integrated scale 
closer to the point of drainage generation.  
The engineering logic of the New Delta / El -Hammam scheme illustrates the scale of ambition involved: 
agricultural wastewater that would historically have discharged through the Rosetta branch of the Nile into the 
Mediterranean is instead redirected roughl y 50 kilometres along the coast, carried underground through large -
diameter pipelines for a further 22 kilometres into the Western Desert, and then conveyed by canal another 42 
kilometres to the treatment plant itself, from where treated water irrigates re claimed land at a reported rate 
on the order of 6 million m³/day at full build -out. Between 2015 and 2020 alone, Egypt reported irrigating 
more than 5,000 km² of previously desert land using this reallocated -drainage model — a scale of land 
reclamation tha t positions drainage reuse as a central pillar of national food -security policy rather than a 
peripheral pilot activity.  
This scale of investment is not incidental. It reflects a structural reality: Egypt's Nile allocation has been fixed 
since the 1959 Nile Waters Agreement while population, urbanisation and irrigated area have all expanded 
several -fold, and hydrological unc ertainty from upstream GERD operations adds a further layer of supply risk 
that drainage reuse is explicitly designed to buffer against. Understanding this policy driver is essential 
background for any project team preparing a comparable report for another  country: the opening section of 
the template should always identify the specific structural constraint — a fixed allocation, a transboundary 
dependency, an aquifer in decline — that makes the proposed WEFE Nexus intervention a policy priority rather 
than merely a technically interesting option.

15.1 Benchmarking Logic  
The USD 670 million envelope used in this report is deliberately positioned as a mid -scale, replicable unit rather 
than a mega -project on the scale of Bahr El -Baqar or New Delta, both of which required national -level financing 
structures led by the Armed F orces Engineering Authority. A decentralised project of this size is intended to be 
financeable by a single multilateral lender or a small blended -finance consortium, and to be repeatable across 
multiple secondary -drain sites in the Delta without requiring  a new national programme each time. When 
adapting this template to another country or site, the CAPEX table should be built bottom -up from unit costs 
(USD per m³/day of treatment capacity, USD per MW of solar, USD per hectare of distribution network) 
benc hmarked against at least one operating reference project, exactly as this report benchmarks against the 
three Egyptian plants.

drainage water reused is a cubic metre that does not need to be drawn from the fixed Nile allocation, and 
every hectare irrigated from treated drain age water is a hectare of reclaimed desert land added to the 
cultivated base without competing for Delta 'old land' water rights. A full feasibility study should quantify this

Page 15 of 21 Operating experience at Al -Mahsama and Bahr El -Baqar offers two specific, transferable risk lessons. The first 
concerns hydraulic risk at intake: drainage flow volumes vary seasonally with the agricultural calendar, and 
pumping -station capacity must be siz ed with sufficient margin to avoid either starving the plant or requiring 
costly emergency discharge — a design parameter that should be explicitly tested during feasibility rather than 
assumed from average annual flow. The second concerns social risk down stream of the intake: shifting 
drainage water away from informal downstream abstraction points, as documented at Al -Mahsama, can 
measurably affect smallholders who rely on that water for supplementary irrigation of high -value crops. This is 
treated in this  report as an operational risk with a financial dimension (compensation, grievance -redress costs, 
reputational risk to lenders) as well as a safeguards issue, and is cross -referenced in Section 20.
